First, the 90-night rule

This decides whether the numbers above are reachable, so it comes before everything else. On our call I called it a council licence. That was wrong, and I want to correct it properly.

What's actually true: in London you can let a home as short-term accommodation for up to 90 nights per calendar year without planning permission. That limit attaches to the property, and it counts nights across every platform and direct bookings combined. Going beyond it needs planning permission for change of use from the council. England has no separate short-let licence. I also said some platforms don't enforce the cap. Please disregard that: the rule applies whichever platform you use, and I wouldn't recommend listing above 90 nights without permission.

Your questions, answered straight

Do I need a licence — and what does the 90-night rule mean for me?
There is no short-let licence in England; the correct route is planning permission for change of use, which you need before letting for more than 90 nights a year in London. Without it, you're limited to 90 nights across all platforms and direct bookings combined. At 90 nights the indicative net is around £18,700, below the long-let figure, so the full income only works with permission. Your freehold, with an attached neighbour that is let out regularly, may be a reasonable candidate, but that's the council's decision, not ours.
What is the honest worst case if the projections aren't hit?
Three things could hurt. First, planning: if permission isn't granted you're capped at 90 nights and short-let doesn't beat a long-let. Second, performance: if you only reached 60% of the run rate, net would be about £36,800, and below about 55% it falls under the long-let figure. Third, there is no guaranteed income: a quiet month is a quiet month, and your mortgage payment is still due until April 2027. We don't guarantee results; the agreement sets out the terms, including how you can end it.
Is £399 a night realistic when comparable places are nearer £100?
£399 is an annual average for a well-presented, well-reviewed property, while the market runs from about £170 to £700+. Places near £100 may be longer-term rents or listings without the same presentation or reviews. We treat the forecast as accurate to within roughly 10–15% either way. Even a rate 20% lower would give about £49,000 net, and short-let only stops beating long-let at around a £218 average rate. We'll re-check it against your photos.
Does the small size and limited storage hurt?
Much less than for a long-let. Long-term tenants accumulate belongings and need storage; short-stay guests bring a suitcase. Two day beds that convert to doubles, a wet room and a small kitchen suit short stays well. It does limit larger groups, which is why we don't expect many contractors, and the nightly rate reflects a compact property.
Who actually stays, and what about damage and wear and tear?
A broad mix: tourists, leisure visitors and some corporate stays, with no single dominant guest type, which is part of why occupancy holds up. Wear and tear is higher than a long-let, but in a flat in this area it isn't a deal-breaker. We photograph the property after every clean and hold a security deposit of typically £250–£500, on top of Airbnb's AirCover. Simple, replaceable decor such as inexpensive prints is exactly what we'd suggest.
Could my garage really help?
Yes, potentially. Free parking is a genuine differentiator in London, helps you rank, and can support a higher rate even when a guest doesn't use it. At about £300 a month, you'd need the uplift in rate and bookings to exceed that, which we think is likely, but we'd test it rather than assume it. Send the details and a photo and we'll factor it in.
