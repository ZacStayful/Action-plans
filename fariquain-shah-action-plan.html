Your plan & next steps

Three corrections from our call

I'd rather you heard these from me than found them in the small print.

  • Cleaning isn't inside the 15%. When you asked whether our fee covers cleaning, I said yes. It covers organising it — the cleaning and laundry itself is a separate cost, paid by the cleaning fee guests are charged on each booking. It's already deducted in every net figure in this plan (modelled at 18% of gross).
  • There is a £42/month software fee and a £1,000 early-exit fee. The presentation summary said "no monthly or exit fee" — that's wrong. The agreement has a £42/month software fee deducted from payouts (already in the figures here), and a £1,000 fee if you end the agreement during the first six months.
  • Fire safety is a legal requirement now, not a future one. I said the rules may change — but a written fire risk assessment is already required under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 for any short-let. Good news: doing it during your renovation is the cheapest time. Details below.

🔥 Fire safety brief — for your builders and architect

This is the guidance I promised on the call. The government guide for small holiday lets is the reference: Making your small paying guest accommodation safe from fire (GOV.UK). It's a checklist to take into those conversations, not a substitute for the assessment itself.

  • Keep sleeping capacity at 10. The small-premises guide covers properties sleeping up to 10 with bedrooms on the ground and first floor only. Five king rooms = 10 guests. This is also why I've left the sofa bed out of the setup quote — adding one would push the cottage into the larger-premises guidance.
  • Check the open-plan layout with your architect. The guide specifically excludes layouts where the only way out from a bedroom passes through an open-plan kitchen or living area. If the stairs will rise from the new open-plan space, ask your architect and building control how the escape route from the first-floor bedrooms is protected. Removing walls and fitting beams needs Building Regulations sign-off anyway, so raise it now.
  • Mains-powered, interlinked smoke alarms (with battery back-up) on the escape route and in living areas, and a heat alarm in the kitchen. Easiest to wire in while the walls are open.
  • Fire blanket and extinguisher in the kitchen; clear, lit escape routes; bedroom windows that can be used for escape where the guide calls for them.
  • Carbon monoxide alarms in any room with a fuel-burning appliance (not needed for the air source heat pump itself).
  • New furniture and furnishings should carry fire-safety labels (Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988) — standard on UK-bought furniture, worth checking on anything sourced through your trade contacts.
  • Electrics: an EICR once the rewire is done, and the hot tub fitted by a qualified electrician.
  • Write the fire risk assessment down before the first guest — it must be recorded in full — and keep it with your testing records.
When we get to the manor, it will need the larger-premises guidance ("Fire safety risk assessment: sleeping accommodation") — worth your architect knowing that now if work on the manor overlaps.

Your questions, answered straight

Where does the £13,700 a month figure come from — is it realistic?

It's built from the booking data of the two most comparable listings within about 6 km. Only two matched because a five-bed cottage on a manor estate is rare — and both of those are currently earning more than we've projected. The wider set of 12 large listings within 8 km averages far higher again, but those are bigger, established houses with years of reviews, so I haven't leaned on them.

I can't tell you yet whether they're closer to Silverstone than you — it's a radius search. That's exactly what the visit after your renovation is for: I'll see the finished cottage, check it against those comparables and firm the figures up or down.

What exactly will I pay, all in?

Platforms: around 15% (Airbnb, Booking.com). Stayful: 15% + VAT on the revenue left after platform fees, plus £42/month software. Cleaning & laundry: a separate cost, funded by the cleaning fee guests pay — modelled at 18% of gross to be safe. Consumables (toiletries, loo roll) restocked at cost.

Every net figure in this plan already has all of that taken off. Direct bookings skip the platform fee entirely, so as the direct share grows, costs fall.

Am I tied in — and what if there are no bookings?

The first six months are a fixed term; leaving inside it costs £1,000. After that it rolls, and either of us can end it with three months' written notice. The notice period exists to honour guests who've already booked, then hand everything — listing, photos, guest details — back to you or your next manager with no overlap.

If you withdraw the property before the notice period ends, the agreement charges the greater of £1,000 or the value of confirmed bookings you'd be cancelling. I'd rather you read that here than discover it.

If there were no bookings, our fee is a percentage of revenue, so there'd be nothing for us to charge except the £42 software fee. Honestly, though, bookings aren't the risk on a property like this — review quality is, and that's what we're built around.

Can my manor cleaners do the cleaning?

Yes, and it probably makes the most sense — they know the estate. The conditions: they need to clean between checkout and the next check-in (often weekends), handle linen to hotel standard, and work from our changeover schedule so they know exactly when a guest has left. I'm happy to speak to them directly.

What about licensing, the council and council tax?

Licensing: England has no short-let licence. The government is introducing a national short-let register — it has said it will be fully running by around March 2027 — so we'll register the cottage once it opens. Nothing to do today.

Council tax: I was right that it doubles — West Northamptonshire has charged a 100% premium on furnished homes that aren't anyone's main residence since April 2025. But I overstated how simply you "get it back". A holiday let can move to business rates once it's been available for at least 140 nights and actually let for at least 70 in a year; until then, budget for the doubled council tax. Once on business rates, small business rate relief can reduce the bill a lot — but because the cottage is company-owned, eligibility depends on what other business property the company holds. One for your accountant.

What insurance do I need? (I didn't answer this on the call)

A specialist holiday-let policy covering buildings, contents and public liability for short-term guests — a standard landlord or home policy usually won't cover it, and the agreement requires it. If there's a mortgage on the cottage, the lender will need to consent to short-letting too. On the call you mentioned mortgages — could you let me know whether the cottage itself carries one? It changes what "covering the mortgage" needs to look like month to month.

What is the honest worst case if projections aren't hit?

The quietest month in the model is July: about £1,975 net in Year 1. That's the floor month, not the norm. If the whole year came in at just 60% of the forecast, you'd net roughly £49,800 in a full year — still around three times the long-let figure.

The real worst case is operational, not financial: a slow start while reviews build. That's why Year 1 is shown at 80%. And if it genuinely didn't work for you, your exit is three months' notice (after the first six), with the listing and photos yours — and selling, your stated fallback, stays open throughout.

Hot tub, EV charger, separate entrance, TVs, the fourth ensuite?

Hot tub: yes — one of the few amenities that lifts the nightly rate in this market because few listings have one. Fourth ensuite: I'd convert the wardrobe room; at this price point, bathrooms matter more than a separate dressing room. EV charger: worth it; pay-per-use commercial chargers exist so guests pay for their own charging — Octopus is a sensible first call. Separate entrance: nice for privacy, not essential — directions through the main drive work fine. TVs: one big screen in the open-plan lounge and one or two in the largest bedrooms, not one per room.
